Emote & badge sizes, for every platform that has them

Checked against official platform documentation · last verified 25 October 2026

Chat emotes are not one image. They are a set of fixed pixel sizes that each platform renders at different zoom levels, and almost every guide online has at least one number wrong — usually by copying the previous guide. This page lists the upload size, the display size, the file limit and the accepted formats for each platform, and says where the number came from.

Sizes are the values each platform actually validates on upload. Where a platform both accepts and displays a different number, both are listed — uploading the display size is the single most common cause of a blurry emote.

Twitch: three sizes, one emote

Twitch is the only platform here that makes you supply every size by hand. One emote is three files: 112×112 for large screens, 56×56 for normal chat and 28×28 for the tiny inline version. If you upload only the 112 file, the upload fails — Twitch treats the set as one asset.

The limits differ by type, which trips people up. A static emote can be a 1 MB PNG. An animated emote is a GIF capped at 60 frames, and each of the three sizes has to come in under 512 KB. Sub badges are a separate, much tighter budget: 25 KB per file at 72×72, 36×36 and 18×18. A badge designed like an emote will almost always be rejected for size, because a 112-pixel emote compresses to something far heavier than 25 KB.

Practical consequence: design at 112×112 with transparency, then scale down. Do not design at 28×28 and scale up. Scaling down averages pixels and keeps the shape readable; scaling up invents pixels and produces the soft, muddy edges that make an emote unreadable in a busy chat.

The 28-pixel test. Before uploading anything, view the artwork at 28×28 on both a light and a dark chat background. If the expression is not readable at that size, no amount of resolution at 112 will save it. Thick outlines and one strong shape beat detail at every size.

7TV: upload big, let the platform cut it down

7TV works the opposite way to Twitch. You upload a single file — up to 7 MB, up to 1000×1000 pixels, up to 1000 frames for animation — and 7TV derives the four render tiers itself: 32×32, 64×64, 96×96 and 128×128. You never export four files.

This is also the number most other guides get wrong. Several popular emote tools state that 7TV caps uploads at 2.5 MB and 150 frames. 7TV's own help centre says 7 MB and 1000 frames. If a tool refuses your file below 7 MB, the tool is enforcing a limit that does not exist.

7TV is also the only one of these platforms that renders wide emotes. The widest accepted ratio is 3:1, which at the 4× tier means 384×128 pixels, with the smaller tiers at 288×96, 192×64 and 96×32. BTTV and FFZ do not display wide emotes — a 384×128 file uploaded there will be squashed into a square.

BetterTTV and FrankerFaceZ: the fixed-size pair

BTTV takes one 112×112 image, under 1 MB, as PNG, GIF or WebP, and displays it at 28 pixels tall in chat. There is no multi-size upload — one file, and the browser scales it.

FFZ uses three tiers: 32×32, 64×64 and 128×128. It is the only platform in this list that accepts wide emotes in its own right, up to 128 pixels wide by 32 pixels tall, which is how the panoramic and text-style emotes on FFZ exist.

Because both platforms display small and scale the single file, start from 112×112 or 128×128 rather than the display size. A 28-pixel source upscaled to 112 is the standard reason an emote looks broken on one platform and fine on another.

Kick

Kick renders emotes at the same 28, 56 and 112 pixel steps as Twitch and accepts uploads larger than the display size, downscaling them on its side. Its upload limits have changed more often than the other platforms here, so treat any exact figure — including the 1 MB in the table above — as a value to confirm on Kick's own dashboard before you rely on it for a batch of emotes.

Discord: emoji and stickers are different things

A Discord emoji is 128×128 and must be under 256 KB, as PNG, JPG or GIF. That size ceiling is the binding constraint — a detailed 128×128 PNG easily exceeds 256 KB, which is why most rejected emoji are rejected for weight, not dimensions.

A server sticker is a separate asset type: exactly 320×320 pixels, under 512 KB, as PNG or APNG, and the animation should stay under about five seconds. Uploading a 320×320 PNG to the emoji slot will not work, and neither will a 128×128 image in the sticker slot — the dimensions are validated, not resized.

Why the numbers disagree across sites

If you compare five guides you will find five different limits, because most of them were written once and copied forward. The rules that actually move are the animated file-size caps and anything involving AVIF or WebP support — those changed as the platforms added formats. The pixel dimensions have been stable for years.

The reliable check is always the upload form itself: it validates before it accepts. If a guide and the upload form disagree, the form wins. The table above is dated so you can tell how stale it is.

Questions

What size should a Twitch emote be?

112×112, 56×56 and 28×28 — all three, every time. Static: PNG up to 1 MB. Animated: GIF, max 60 frames, each size under 512 KB.

What size are Twitch sub badges?

72×72, 36×36 and 18×18, at 25 KB per file. The 25 KB ceiling is the reason badge uploads fail far more often than emote uploads.

What is the 7TV emote size limit?

7 MB, 1000×1000 pixels, 1000 frames, in PNG, APNG, WEBP, WEBM or GIF — per 7TV's own upload documentation. It renders at 32, 64, 96 and 128, and you do not export those yourself.

What size does BetterTTV need?

One 112×112 file, under 1 MB, PNG/GIF/WebP. Displayed at 28 pixels tall.

What sizes does FrankerFaceZ use?

32×32, 64×64 and 128×128, plus wide emotes up to 128×32.

What size should a Discord emoji be?

128×128 and under 256 KB. Stickers are 320×320, under 512 KB, PNG or APNG.

Should I upload the biggest size or export every size?

Twitch, Kick and BTTV: export every size yourself. 7TV and FFZ: upload the largest and let the platform generate the rest. In both cases start from artwork at least as large as the biggest tier — downscaling preserves detail, upscaling cannot create it.
